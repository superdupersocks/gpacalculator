Is a 1.8 GPA good?

No. A 1.8 is well below average: C− work, about 72%, against a national average of 3.11 for 2019 graduates (NAEP High School Transcript Study). It is under the 2.0 that many colleges set as a minimum, so raising it is the priority.

In high school

Most four-year colleges will not admit a student below 2.0. Use the time you have left in high school to raise it; community colleges are open-admission and a common route to a four-year degree.

In college

In college a GPA below 2.0 usually means academic probation, which can affect financial aid. Talk to an academic advisor about a plan.

Weighted or unweighted?

These figures are for the unweighted 4.0 scale. If your school gives extra points for Honors, AP or IB classes, your weighted GPA can be higher than 1.8. Colleges usually recalculate GPAs their own way, so they see both. Work out yours with the Weighted GPA calculator.

What a 1.8 GPA means for college

With a 1.8 most four-year colleges will not admit you directly. Community colleges are open-admission, and many students transfer to a four-year college after earning good grades there.

If you are still in high school, every semester of better grades counts. Ask your counselor about credit recovery for classes you failed.

Freshman

Sophomore

You are about halfway through, and raising a 1.8 is still realistic. Focus first on the classes where you are closest to the next grade, and turn in every assignment. Colleges also notice an upward trend.

Junior

Junior-year grades are the last full year most colleges see before you apply, so they count. Even if your overall GPA moves only a little, strong junior grades show an upward trend. Ask your counselor about retaking classes you failed.

Senior

Your GPA will not move much before applications, so plan around it: apply to colleges whose requirements you meet, including community colleges, and keep your senior grades up, because colleges see them and some offers depend on them.

What helps

Now that you know where you stand it’s a good idea to think about ways to raise your GPA.  

  1. Identify Your Challenges: Chances are some classes are harder for you than others.  Do you struggle in early morning classes?  Or classes that require a lot of reading?  Is math a struggle for you?  Try to figure out what’s really dragging down your grades and then make a plan to fix the problem.  
  2. Plan Your Schedule Well: It can be overwhelming to choose classes each semester, but if possible, don’t overload yourself with work.  You’ll be much more likely to get a good GPA if you steer away from advanced classes and take a light course load. 
  3. Join a Study Group: Sometimes a study group can be just the accountability you need to get your grades on track. Make a group with others in your class or reach out to a counselor or teacher to find out if any groups are already up and running. 

1.8 GPA – Frequently asked questions

What does a 1.8 GPA indicate?

A 1.8 GPA indicates below-average academic performance, generally corresponding to 'C-' grades in your courses. This level suggests a need for considerable improvement in understanding and engaging with your coursework.

Is a 1.8 GPA good enough for college admissions?

A 1.8 GPA is below the typical requirements for most colleges, but some institutions may consider applicants with lower GPAs, especially those with holistic admissions processes or special admission programs for students who show potential for improvement. Strengthening your application with details of personal growth, extracurricular involvements, and strong letters of recommendation can help compensate for a lower GPA.

How can I improve a 1.8 GPA?

Improving a 1.8 GPA involves a dedicated effort to score higher in future classes and, when possible, retaking previously low-performing courses to better those grades. Key to this improvement are enhanced study habits, seeking academic assistance, and prioritizing your coursework to increase your academic standing.

What scholarship opportunities are available with a 1.8 GPA?

While a 1.8 GPA may significantly limit access to scholarships based on academic merit, there are still financial support options that consider broader criteria. Look for scholarships based on community service, personal achievements, overcoming obstacles, or specific talents. Additionally, some scholarships target students who demonstrate the determination to succeed academically despite facing earlier challenges.
